\section{Characterizing zkML Serving Workloads}
\label{sec:characterization}
This section characterizes the properties of partitioned zkML workloads that shape scheduling decisions in a shared prover. We focus on four questions. How does partition granularity affect the size, runtime, memory demand, and available parallelism of proof jobs? How does resource usage evolve over a job's execution rather than only at its peak? How much circuit-specific state can be reused across repeated executions? And, when requests overlap, how much concurrency can be admitted without exceeding available memory?

We answer these questions across multiple model families and partitioning configurations. First, we quantify how partition granularity changes job cost and the amount of parallel work exposed by a single inference. We then profile memory usage over the lifetime of individual jobs and test whether these profiles are stable across repeated executions of the same circuit. Next, we measure the effect of reusing circuit-specific setup state by comparing cold and warm executions. Finally, we evaluate concurrent execution and quantify the additional overlap enabled by accounting for stage-level resource demand rather than reserving each job's peak memory for its entire lifetime.

\subsection{Workloads and Measurement Method}
\label{sec:characterization-method}
Our characterization uses TinyBERT, MobileNetV2, and NanoGPT (4L),
spanning convolutional and transformer architectures with both encoder
and autoregressive models. Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes their model
and proving characteristics. We export each model as an ONNX computation
graph and partition it at the finest granularity considered in our
experiments, producing 120, 100, and 181 proof jobs, respectively.
Coarser groupings are used where needed to study how partition size
changes the resulting workload.

We compare monolithic proving, where the full model is encoded as a
single circuit, with partitioned proving, where all proof jobs are
executed sequentially on the same prover. This keeps execution serial in
both cases and lets us attribute differences to proving granularity
rather than inter-job parallelism. Both configurations use
EZKL~\cite{ezkl} on a machine with two AMD EPYC 9354 processors and
755~GiB of RAM. We also evaluate NanoGPT (10L) in a separate scaling
experiment to examine whether partitioning makes a larger transformer
model feasible to prove.

For each proof job, we record execution time, process memory usage, and
transitions between major proof-construction stages. These measurements
capture variation across jobs and over time within a job, allowing us to
distinguish sustained demand from short-lived peaks. Unless otherwise
stated, runs begin from a cold state, with reusable setup artifacts
regenerated to capture the full proof-construction lifecycle.

\subsection{Impact of Partitioning on Proving Cost}
\label{sec:decomposition-benefit}

Across all three workloads, partitioning reduces end-to-end proving time
even when the resulting proof jobs are executed serially on the same
prover. Figure~\ref{fig:partition-benefits} compares monolithic request
completion time with partitioned request completion time under serial
execution. Relative to monolithic proving, request completion time
decreases by 40.6\% for TinyBERT, 16.3\% for MobileNetV2, and 60.5\%
for NanoGPT (4L). Thus, despite introducing additional prover
invocations and partition boundaries, decomposing a model into smaller
circuits can reduce the total proving time required to complete an
inference request even without inter-job parallelism.

Partitioning also substantially reduces peak memory. Relative to
monolithic proving, peak memory falls by 80.5\% for TinyBERT, 91.5\%
for MobileNetV2, and 98.0\% for NanoGPT (4L). Under sequential
partitioned execution, the prover need only accommodate the largest
individual proof job rather than the full monolithic circuit. This
reduction is particularly important for larger models. For example,
monolithic proving of NanoGPT (10L) exhausts available memory, whereas
partitioned execution completes with a peak memory usage of only
12.0~GiB.

\textbf{Implication.}
Partitioning changes the workload presented to the runtime, not only by
exposing additional parallelism but also by reducing the cost and memory
footprint of individual proof jobs. It can also make models feasible that
cannot be proved monolithically on the same hardware. The resulting
proof jobs, however, are highly uneven in both runtime and memory demand,
which we characterize next.

\subsection{Heterogeneity Across Proof Jobs}
\label{sec:job-heterogeneity}
Figure~\ref{fig:job-heterogeneity} shows the empirical distributions of
proof-job execution time and peak memory across the three workloads.
All three exhibit substantial heterogeneity. TinyBERT is the most
extreme case. Its longest-running job takes 1,461.30~s compared with a
median of 4.47~s, a \(327\times\) difference, and accounts for 40.9\%
of aggregate job execution time. MobileNetV2 and NanoGPT (4L) show the
same pattern at smaller magnitude, with their longest-running jobs
approximately \(21\times\) and \(31\times\) slower than the
corresponding medians.

This skew means that request progress is not well represented by the
number of unfinished jobs. A request with only a few jobs remaining may
still contain much of its proving work, while a request with many
remaining jobs may consist largely of short proofs. Exposing many proof
jobs therefore does not by itself guarantee a short request completion
time when a small subset lies on the dominant execution path.

Memory demand is similarly uneven. Median per-job peak memory is
0.21, 3.38, and 0.22~GiB for TinyBERT, MobileNetV2, and NanoGPT (4L),
respectively, while the largest observed peaks reach 51.61, 41.45, and
5.70~GiB. As Figure~\ref{fig:job-heterogeneity} shows, most jobs require
far less memory than the largest jobs in the same workload. A fixed
concurrency limit therefore provides little indication of actual memory
pressure, since the same number of active jobs can correspond to very
different aggregate memory requirements.

\textbf{Implication.}
Runtime and memory constrain scheduling in different ways. Runtime
estimates help identify proof jobs that may dominate request completion,
while memory estimates determine which jobs can safely share a prover
node. A scheduler that reasons only about the number of active jobs
captures neither effect. We next examine whether memory demand also
changes within a proof job, further affecting which work can safely
overlap.

\subsection{Resource Demand Across Execution Stages}
\label{sec:stage-resource-demand}

Peak memory determines whether a proof job can execute on a node, but it
does not reveal how memory demand evolves over the course of execution.
We therefore sample process memory throughout each proof and align the
samples with three high-level stages of the proving pipeline.
\emph{Preparation} includes circuit setup and request-specific witness
generation, \emph{key generation} constructs the circuit's proving and
verification keys, and \emph{proving} generates the proof using the
witness and proving key. This lets us compare stage-level demand with the job's whole-execution
peak.

Figure~\ref{fig:stage-memory} shows the highest-memory proof job from
each workload. In all three cases, memory demand is substantially lower
during preparation than during key generation and proving. TinyBERT
rises from below 6.38~GiB during preparation to 51.61~GiB during
proving, while MobileNetV2 rises from below 0.92~GiB to 41.45~GiB.
NanoGPT (4L) shows the same pattern at smaller scale, reaching
5.70~GiB during proving. Table~\ref{tab:temporal-memory} quantifies this variation. Arocss all 
TinyBERT, MobileNetV2, and NanoGPT jobs, they spend only 4.8\%, 3.5\%, and
13.3\% of execution at or above 90\% of peak memory. Their peaks are
also \(4.07\times\), \(2.53\times\), and \(2.22\times\) their
time-averaged memory consumption, respectively.

\textbf{Implication.}
Whole-job peak memory is therefore a conservative basis for admission
throughout execution. Stage-aware admission could expose additional
concurrency by allowing lower-memory work to proceed while delaying
memory-intensive stages until sufficient capacity is available. This
requires predicting stage demand before execution, which we examine
next.
